STORY OF THE TOURNAMENT
----------------------------------------------------------------------

The 9th FLLA World Cup brought the tournament back to Ayuma after the
inaugural 1700 edition had been staged in Atirha. Emara, awarded the hosting
rights at the 1701 Council session, built a new National Arena in the federal
capital and an accompanying network of provincial venues for the group stage.
It was a competition the home support arrived expecting to win.

They got their wish — but only just. The 3.09 goals-per-match average made
the 1704 edition the most freely-scoring FLLA World Cup since the inaugural
tournament, with goals flowing through the closing stages on the back of
technically polished attacking play. Defensive structures were beaten less
by force than by movement and combinations in the final third — a hallmark
of what FLLA observers would later codify as the modern Emaran style.

Emara navigated the bracket with a champion-elect's composure: top of Group A,
through the quarter-finals and a tightly-contested semi-final, into the Final
as overwhelming favourites against Siana.

The Final at the National Arena was decided in the opening half hour. Emara
struck inside the first ten minutes, doubled their lead before the break, and
made it 3-0 with a third early in the second period. Siana responded with
two of their own through the closing twenty minutes — the Sianan attack
refusing to give up even as the clock ran down — but Emara held on for a
3-2 victory. The defence of that early lead, more than the goals that
created it, would become the match Emaran coaches pointed back to for decades
as the foundation of the country's modern Arraby tradition.

Individual honours went to the Sianan attacking talisman — beaten in the Final
but the tournament's outstanding goalscorer with 9 strikes across the campaign.
His tournament drew comparison to Sándor Kocsis at Earth's 1954 World Cup,
where the Hungarian forward had won the Golden Boot despite his side losing the
final. That 1954 edition's 3-2 final scoreline was an oft-cited point of contrast
in the post-tournament press, the two finals separated by a world but reached
at almost exactly the same numerical conclusion.
